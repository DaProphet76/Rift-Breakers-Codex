# Chapter One

## The Last Train East

### A journey begins.

On the morning Darius Vale decided to carry his family out of New Babylonia, the eastern wall was hidden behind fog.

This seemed to him a poor sign.

He stood in the narrow street with a travel chest balanced against one knee and stared toward the place where the wall ought to have been. The fog had swallowed the watchtowers, the rusted roofs, and even the high lamps that burned all night along the district boundary. It left only the sounds: wheels knocking over broken stones, vendors calling over one another, and the deep iron groan of the city waking against its will.

From inside the house came his wife's voice.

“If you are waiting for the road to improve,” Lysandra called, “you should sit down.”

Darius looked at the chest. He looked at the door. “I am considering whether we truly require three blankets.”

“We require four.”

“There are only three.”

“Then we require all of them.”

Darius sighed and lifted the chest. It had been repaired so often that almost none of the original wood remained. One brass corner had come loose during the night and now bit into his palm.

“You might carry the light end,” he said as Lysandra appeared in the doorway.

“I am carrying the important end.”

She rested both hands on the round curve beneath her coat. Six months of pregnancy had changed the way she stood, slept, walked, and regarded chairs. It had not changed the steady look in her dark eyes.

Darius set down the chest. “You should be inside.”

“I was inside. The chest did not become lighter.”

“That is because you put the cooking pot in it.”

“You said the eastern settlements have cooking pots.”

“I said they have kitchens.”

“A kitchen without my pot is merely a room where someone has made a mistake.”

Darius had no answer to that. Marriage had taught him that there were battles a wise man avoided and battles he lost more quickly to save time.

A small face appeared beside Lysandra's coat. Their daughter had dressed herself for the journey. This meant that one boot was properly tied, the other was not tied at all, and her blue scarf had been wound around her neck enough times to threaten her breathing.

“Can we see the sea from Eastmere?” the girl asked.

“Not from the town,” Darius said.

“Mirella says you can smell it.”

“Mirella says many things.”

“Most of them are true,” Lysandra said.

“That is what makes her dangerous.”

The girl considered this. “Will there be Rakers?”

Lysandra's hand moved from her stomach to the child's shoulder.

Darius crouched before his daughter. “Not where we are going.”

It was the answer fathers gave when truth had no useful shape. The settlements beyond the eastern line were said to be safer. They were also said to have clean wells, open fields, honest officials, and birds large enough to carry away badly behaved children. Darius believed two of these things, though which two changed according to his mood.

“Rakers do not like the sea,” he added.

“Why?”

“They cannot swim.”

“Neither can you.”

Lysandra turned away, but not before Darius saw her smile.

“Go and fetch your little bag,” he told the girl. “Then we will collect Mirella and Asher.”

Their daughter hurried inside. Darius watched her go, then lowered his voice.

“We will be through the east gate before midday.”

“If the inspector honours the permits.”

“He will.”

“You paid for passage,” Lysandra said. “You did not pay for honour.”

Darius touched the inner pocket of his coat. The permits were still there, folded in waxed cloth: two adult passages, one child passage, and a dependent mark for the baby not yet born. Beside them lay another pair bearing Mirella's name and Asher's. They had cost him the savings of eight years, Lysandra's silver comb, and a favour he did not expect to survive repaying.

“The seals are genuine,” he said.

“So was the silver comb.”

“Your mother gave you that comb.”

“Which is why I know what it was worth.”

He reached for her hand. Her fingers were cold.

“Are you frightened?” he asked.

“Yes.”

The honesty of it struck him harder than reassurance would have.

Lysandra looked along the street. Families had begun to emerge from patched houses, carrying bundles to workshops or pails to ration pumps. No one carried a travel chest. A chest invited questions; a chest with an eastern rail mark invited envy.

“I am frightened of staying,” she said. “That is why we are leaving.”

Darius squeezed her hand once. Then he lifted the chest, and they began.

Mirella Renn lived three lanes north in a house that leaned toward its neighbour as if sharing a secret. Its lower walls were old fitted stone; everything above them had been made from timber, sheet metal, and stubbornness. A line of washed cloth hung across the front room, turning the windows pale blue and green.

Mirella was at her worktable when they arrived. Morning light fell over heaps of mended coats and trousers. She held a needle between her teeth while she tied a parcel with both hands.

“You are late,” she said around the needle.

“The sun has barely risen,” Darius replied.

Mirella removed the needle. “The sun is not catching a train.”

She was small and straight-backed, with black hair pinned above her neck and a pale scar that passed through one eyebrow. Darius had known her for nine years and had never learned how she received the scar. He had asked once. Mirella had said, “Carelessly,” in a tone that ended the matter.

Asher sat beneath the table with six painted wooden animals arranged around him. At three years old he possessed solemn grey eyes, unmanageable dark hair, and the firm belief that furniture had been built chiefly to provide hiding places.

Darius's daughter dropped to her knees. “That is not a horse,” she told him.

Asher held up the red animal.

“It has horns.”

“Horse,” he said.

“It is a cow.”

Asher placed it beside a blue dog. “Horse.”

The girl looked to the adults for justice.

“It is his animal,” Mirella said. “It may pursue any profession it chooses.”

Darius set down the chest. “That explains the city council.”

Lysandra gave him a warning look. The outer districts had thin walls, and officials often had thinner tempers.

Mirella slid the tied parcel into a canvas bag. Only one bag waited beside the door.

“Where is the rest?” Darius asked.

“There is no rest.”

He looked around the room. “You cannot mean to leave everything.”

“I mean to carry Asher. Therefore, I must leave almost everything.”

“I can carry him,” Darius said.

“You have the chest.”

“I can leave the pot.”

“You cannot,” Lysandra said.

Mirella nodded gravely. “Then civilization has spoken.”

The girl's laughter made Asher smile, though he could not have understood the joke. Mirella's expression softened when she looked at him. She knelt, buttoned his little brown coat, and tucked the red wooden animal into his pocket.

“Horse,” Asher said.

“A splendid one,” Mirella replied.

She rose and drew on her own coat. It was plain wool, worn shiny at the cuffs. Around her neck hung a small oval pendant on a dark cord. The metal was old and dull, marked with lines too shallow for Darius to read. Mirella slipped it beneath her collar.

Lysandra noticed. “You kept it.”

Mirella's fingers remained at her throat. “It belonged to my mother.”

“I thought you sold everything she left you.”

“Not everything.”

The two women held each other's gaze. Whatever passed between them was not meant for Darius. He busied himself with retightening the chest straps.

Outside, a bell sounded from the eastern checkpoint: three slow notes followed by two quick ones.

Mirella went still.

“Inspection change,” Darius said.

“Early,” she replied.

“We have permits.”

“Permits are paper. Inspectors are men.”

“I have found paper more reliable.”

“That is because paper has never wanted a bribe.”

Lysandra helped Asher into his wool cap. “Then we had better reach the station before the men discover new appetites.”

They left together: Darius with the chest, Mirella with the canvas bag and Asher, Lysandra holding her daughter's hand. The children began the journey walking. Before they reached the end of the lane, Asher asked to be carried. Mirella lifted him without complaint.

The road to Eastgate Station passed through the oldest part of the outer district. Here the houses had been raised among the bones of buildings from before the Sundering. Smooth black pillars stood beside crooked chimneys. Broken stone figures watched from walls whose upper floors had vanished generations ago. People had built shops beneath them, hung laundry from them, and forgotten whom the statues represented.

Above the rooftops, the wall emerged from the thinning fog.

It did not protect the outer district. Everyone who lived there understood this, though the maps painted the wall in the same comforting grey that surrounded the richer rings. The eastern wall protected New Babylonia from those already outside it. The people of the fringe had merely been permitted to live in its shadow.

They joined a growing line near the checkpoint. Guards in dark coats stood beneath iron arches, checking travel papers and opening luggage. A woman ahead of them argued while an inspector emptied flour from her sack onto the stones.

“There is nothing in it,” the woman said.

“Then you lose nothing by the search,” the inspector replied.

The flour spread white around his boots.

Darius felt Lysandra's fingers close around his sleeve.

“Do not argue,” she whispered.

“I had no intention.”

“Your face is arguing.”

He tried to arrange it into innocence. Lysandra shook her head.

When their turn came, Inspector Vey stood before them. He was broad through the shoulders and clean at the collar, two qualities rarely found together in the outer ring. A brass badge shone on his chest. His eyes moved over the travel chest, Lysandra's belly, Mirella's bag, and both children.

“Destination?” Vey asked.

“Eastmere,” Darius said.

“Purpose?”

“Resettlement.”

“Occupation?”

“Millwright.”

Vey looked at Mirella. “Yours?”

“Seamstress.”

“Eastmere has seamstresses.”

Mirella met his gaze. “Then its people must have clothes.”

Darius closed his eyes briefly.

Vey held out one gloved hand. “Papers.”

Darius passed them over. The inspector studied each seal, then held the documents up to the grey light.

“These were issued yesterday.”

“Yes,” Darius said.

“Travel allocation closed five days ago.”

“These are labour exemptions.”

“I can read.”

No one answered. Even Mirella understood when silence was the better weapon.

Vey examined the dependent mark. “One unborn child.”

Lysandra's expression cooled. “At present.”

The inspector looked at her for a long moment. Then his gaze dropped to the girl beside her.

Vey checked the third permit, then looked down at the girl. She slipped her hand into her father's and hid partly behind his coat.

The inspector turned to Asher. “And this one?”

“Asher Renn,” Mirella said.

“His father?”

“Dead.”

The word landed without drama. Mirella had long ago worn away its sharp edges, at least in public.

Vey folded the permits. For one dreadful moment, Darius thought the man meant to pocket them. Instead, he stamped the corner of each page and returned them.

“Train Seven,” the inspector said. “Freight platform. Departure at the second noon bell. If the gate seals before then, the permits expire.”

“Why would the gate seal?” Lysandra asked.

Vey glanced toward the fog beyond the wall. “Because gates do.”

He waved them through.

Darius did not breathe freely until they had crossed the inner yard.

“Your face was still arguing,” Lysandra told him.

“My face won.”

“Inspector Vey let us pass.”

“A narrow victory is still a victory.”

Mirella shifted Asher on her hip. “Try not to celebrate until we are beyond rifle range.”

Train Seven waited on the last platform, twelve weather-beaten cars behind a squat iron engine. Steam curled around its wheels. Porters loaded sacks, tool crates, cages of chickens, and the possessions of people who had mistaken the word *resettlement* for an invitation to bring their entire lives.

The platform smelled of coal smoke, damp wool, and fear.

Darius presented their papers to the conductor, a narrow man named Pell who wore a red cap and the expression of someone personally offended by passengers.

“Car nine,” Pell said. “One chest. Two hand parcels.”

“We have one hand parcel,” Darius replied.

Pell marked his slate. “Then you may feel proud of your restraint.”

“How long to Eastmere?” Mirella asked.

“Two days if the line is clear. Four if it isn't.”

“And if the line is gone?”

Pell looked at her. “Then the journey becomes shorter.”

He moved to the next family.

They found places in car nine on two facing wooden benches. The children took the window. The girl wiped soot from the glass with her sleeve and peered toward the wall. Asher climbed beside her, drew the red animal from his pocket, and made it gallop along the sill.

“Cow,” the girl reminded him.

“Horse.”

“It cannot be a horse merely because you say so.”

Asher pushed it toward her. “Horse.”

The girl studied the wooden creature, then nodded. “Very well. But it is an ugly horse.”

Asher beamed.

Across from them, Lysandra eased herself onto the bench. Her face tightened.

“Pain?” Mirella asked.

“The baby objecting to the seat.”

Darius crouched before her. “Is it—”

“No,” Lysandra said. “The little one is kicking me. That is all.”

“You said it might be a daughter.”

“A daughter would have better manners.”

Mirella placed a folded blanket behind Lysandra's back. “There. If the child complains now, he may address me directly.”

Lysandra smiled, but her hand caught Mirella's before she could withdraw it.

“You could still stay,” Lysandra said quietly. “No one would blame you.”

Mirella looked at Asher. “He has learned to call a cow a horse. The city has nothing left to teach him.”

“I mean it.”

“So do I.”

Lysandra lowered her voice. “Eastmere may not be what they promised.”

“Nothing ever is.”

“You are leaving your home.”

“A home is where your child sleeps without learning the difference between warning bells.” Mirella glanced toward the platform. “Asher knows all six.”

Darius sat beside his wife. Beyond the soot-streaked window, guards began closing the baggage doors. Families called farewells across the platform. Some wept. Others did not. There are sorrows too old for tears, and the outer district had collected more than its share.

The first noon bell rang.

The engine answered with a hiss.

Asher covered his ears. The girl laughed, then covered hers as the whistle shrieked overhead.

“One more bell,” Darius said. “Then we are away.”

He spoke with such confidence that, for a moment, they believed him.

### The world tears open.

Rain began as the first noon bell faded. It came softly at first, tapping against the carriage roof and stippling the soot on the windows.

*Tap. Tap-tap. Tap.*

Asher pressed one finger to the glass and followed a droplet as it wandered downward. Beside him, the girl made the wooden animal trot along the sill. Around them, Train Seven settled into its ordinary noises: the low *chuff-chuff* of the engine, the knock of pipes beneath the floor, the creak of passengers shifting on hard benches.

Outside, three lean dogs nosed through spilled grain beneath the freight ramp. The largest stopped eating. Its head rose, ears pointed toward the rails.

A second dog noticed. Then the third.

The engine exhaled with a long *hissss* of steam. When the sound faded, Mirella heard something beneath it.

*Scritch. Scritch-scritch.*

The noise travelled below the carriage, crossed under their feet, and continued toward the eastern wall. It was too deliberate for rats and too deep beneath the stone for anything that should have been alive.

Mirella straightened. “Did you hear that?”

Darius tilted his head. “Hear what?”

The sound came again.

*Scrrrape.*

This time the whole car heard it. Conversation weakened. A porter outside lowered the crate in his arms. The largest dog backed away from the rail, growling into the ground.

“Mama?” Asher whispered.

Mirella drew him from the window. “Come here.”

The rain quickened.

*Tap-tap-tap-tap.*

One of the blue station lamps flickered. *Bzzzt.* The next lamp answered, then the next, until cold blue light raced down the platform. The engine coughed and lost its rhythm. Somewhere inside its iron body, a piston struck once—*clank*—and went still.

The silence that followed did not arrive all at once. It spread outward as people noticed one missing sound after another: no engine, no porters, no conversation. Even the rain seemed quieter.

Then something struck beneath the station.

*THUMM.*

The floor jumped. Luggage fell from the racks. The dogs erupted into barking and fled west, chains skipping across the stones.

*THUMM.*

A black crack raced across the pavement beyond the freight ramp. It split a paving stone, cut through one rail, and curved beneath the shadow of the eastern wall. Blue-white light bled from its edges.

People nearest the crack stumbled away.

“Clear the platform!” a guard shouted.

The line of light continued bending until it formed a great half-circle across the ground. With a sound like ice tearing across a winter lake—*krrrraaaak*—the glowing arc lifted upright.

The air inside it changed.

The station remained visible around the arch, but within its boundary lay another place: a churning darkness filled with storm clouds, drifting ash, and distant points of orange fire. Pale energy crawled around the rim in branching flashes. Each flash struck the pavement with a sharp *snap*, throwing chips of stone into the rain.

It was not a hole in the ground.

It was a doorway standing upon it.

Shapes gathered on the other side.

The second noon bell began to ring.

*BONG—*

The surface inside the arch bulged outward. A grey limb broke through. Then another. The creature passed across the threshold in a low rush and hit the platform on all fours.

Most passengers stared because, until that instant, a Raker had been no more than a word spoken during warnings or used to frighten children away from broken walls. Now the word had a shape.

The creature stood no taller than a grown child, but there was nothing young about it. Grey hide stretched over a narrow frame. Its legs bent backward like those of a starved hound. Long fingers scraped the stones with hooked black nails—*tik-tik-tik*—while its smooth head turned toward the nearest movement. Where eyes should have been were two wet hollows. Its lipless mouth opened and released a rapid, hungry clicking.

*Kik-kik-kik-kik.*

An old woman near the baggage carts dropped her parcel.

“Raker,” she breathed.

The name travelled faster than understanding.

“Raker!” someone shouted. “Rakers!”

The creature sprang.

A guard fired. *Crack!* The shot tore through one shoulder, spinning the Raker sideways, but it struck the guard before he could fire again. They crashed into the base of the bell tower. A support split with a sharp *KRAK*. Stone groaned overhead.

“Down!” Darius shouted.

The bell tower folded across the platform.

*BOOOOM.*

Bronze, stone, and broken timber struck the train. Windows burst inward—*krrash!*—and the carriage rocked on its wheels. Darius threw himself over his daughter. Mirella pulled Asher beneath the bench while Lysandra braced both hands against the seat.

Dust rolled through the broken windows. People coughed and cried out. Through the grey cloud came the scrape of claws and that eager clicking.

*Kik-kik-kik.*

Another shape burst from the glowing doorway. Then two more. The Rakers poured onto the platform, rushing whatever moved, scaling posts and carriage walls with hooked hands. One leapt onto a roof with a hollow *whump*. Another vanished beneath a freight wagon after a fleeing porter.

Mirella covered Asher's face.

“Those things cannot cross the wall,” the girl whispered.

Darius drew her against him. “They have.”

The gateway pulsed.

*WHUMM.*

Its blue-white rim flared, illuminating a much larger shape waiting beyond the storm-dark surface.

The Rakers felt it before the people did. Their clicking weakened. Several flattened themselves against roofs and stones, fingers digging in as they bowed. One abandoned a fallen victim and crawled away from the gateway.

A long, colourless hand reached through.

The being that followed did not rush. It walked.

It was taller than any man on the platform and narrow in a way that suggested neither weakness nor hunger. Tattered darkness hung from its shoulders like robes drowned in smoke. Beneath those folds, pale armour clung to its body in uneven plates, each rimmed with white frost. Its face remained hidden within a deep hood, though two faint points of winter-blue light watched from inside.

Its first foot touched the pavement.

*Crisssh.*

Frost spread beneath it.

The white stain raced through cracks in the stone and climbed the freight ramp. A burning baggage cart hissed—*ssssss*—as the flames died. Steam folded into smoke. Nearby windows clouded from their edges inward, then cracked beneath the cold.

While the Rakers attacked whatever ran nearest, the greater creature searched. Its hooded face moved from one packed carriage to the next with dreadful patience. It knew its prey was near, but not yet where.

Three guards formed a line near the gatehouse.

“Fire!”

*Crack-crack-crack!*

Bullets struck the pale armour with hard metallic blows—*pang, pang, pang*. Chips of ice and blackened metal broke away. One shot drove the creature half a step backward.

It could be hurt.

The guards did not have time to fire another full volley.

The entity extended one hand.

Cold swept across the platform in a white rush. The first guard's rifle froze to his gloves. Frost climbed his sleeves and covered his face before he could release the weapon. The second fell to his knees, drawing one short breath that left ice across his lips. The third tried to run. He managed four steps before the cold took his legs and brought him down.

None of them moved again.

The damaged plates across the creature's chest remained cracked, but fresh frost thickened over them.

It resumed searching.

The pendant beneath Mirella's collar turned cold.

The chill passed through her dress and settled against her skin like a piece of winter sharpened to an edge. She caught the pendant through the cloth and stared toward the gateway.

“Darius,” she said. “We must move.”

Rifles answered from the gatehouse—*crack-crack-crack!* One creature spun and fell. The others scattered so quickly that the next shots struck carriage walls with sharp *pings* and sent passengers diving for cover.

“Hold your fire!” Inspector Vey shouted somewhere outside. “Seal the gate!”

The great doors in the eastern wall began to close.

“No,” Darius said.

He climbed over the bench and shoved at the carriage door. It did not move.

“It is locked,” Mirella said.

“They are sealing us inside.”

“They are sealing those things inside too.”

The engine whistle gave two sharp blasts. The cars jerked, rolling half a wheel before the brakes screamed. Through the window, Darius saw a fallen section of bell tower across the forward track.

Train Seven would not be leaving by that line.

Something landed on the carriage roof with a crushing *WHAM*.

The metal bent above them.

Passengers cried out and pushed toward the rear door. The old man from across the aisle raised his walking stick as if he meant to reason with the roof.

“Stay away from the windows!” Mirella shouted.

A grey arm punched through the glass—*KRRASH!* Shards scattered across the benches.

Its claws caught the old man's coat and dragged him against the broken frame. Darius seized the walking stick, drove its iron tip into the creature's wrist, and twisted. The Raker clicked in fury. Mirella pulled the old man free as the arm withdrew.

“That was a good stick,” the old man gasped.

“I will buy you another,” Darius said.

“With what?” Lysandra demanded.

“This is not the moment to discuss our finances.”

The Raker struck the roof again. Rivets snapped loose.

At the rear of the car, someone found the emergency latch. The door slid open, and the crowd poured onto the track bed.

“Do not follow them,” Mirella said.

Darius looked through the shattered window. Two Rakers had already turned toward the fleeing passengers.

“Then where?” he asked.

Mirella pointed toward the front of the train. “The service trench. It runs beneath the platform to the east maintenance yard.”

“The gate is closing.”

“The trench passes under it.”

Lysandra stared at her. “How do you know?”

“I repaired uniforms for the trench crews.”

“They told you the route?” Darius asked.

“Men remove their maps before removing their trousers. They are not equally careful with conversation.”

Even then, with monsters on the roof and death on the platform, Darius almost laughed.

Mirella took Asher. Darius lifted his daughter and helped Lysandra down from the car. They moved between the train and the platform, sheltered for the moment by the wheels.

The service-trench hatch lay thirty paces ahead.

Between them and the hatch stood Inspector Vey.

He held a short rifle in both hands. His clean collar was red with blood—not his own, Darius thought. Vey fired beneath a baggage cart. A Raker shrieked and scrambled away on three limbs.

“Back in the cars!” Vey ordered.

“The track is blocked,” Darius said. “We need the trench.”

“The trench gate is locked.”

“You have keys.”

Vey looked toward the main gate. It was halfway closed. Beyond it lay open land, the eastern road, and the promise for which they had paid everything.

“The trench must remain sealed,” the inspector said.

Mirella stared at him. “To protect whom?”

Vey did not answer.

A Raker climbed onto the platform behind him.

The girl saw it first. “Mister!”

Vey turned and fired. The shot struck the creature in the shoulder but did not stop it. Darius swung the old man's walking stick. The iron tip caught the Raker across the jaw. Mirella drove her sewing shears into the side of its neck.

The creature thrashed. Its claws ripped Vey's coat and opened the flesh beneath. He fell against the platform edge. Darius struck again until the Raker rolled onto the rails, clicking and twitching.

For a moment, no one moved.

Then Vey pulled a ring of keys from his belt and shoved it at Darius.

“Third key,” he said through clenched teeth. “Blue mark.”

Darius took them. “Come with us.”

“I have a gate to close.”

“The gate is closing itself.”

Vey looked toward the station, where his guards were firing among the crowd. “Gates do nothing by themselves.”

He raised his rifle and limped away.

The trench hatch opened on the third key.

Darius descended first, then helped Lysandra onto the ladder. Their daughter followed. Mirella handed Asher down and came last, pulling the hatch shut as claws scraped across the platform above.

The trench was scarcely wide enough for two people. Pipes crowded its walls, sweating hot water. Red lamps glowed at long intervals. The air tasted of rust and coal smoke.

They hurried east.

Behind them, metal shrieked—*skreeee!*—as the hatch tore open.

“Do not look back,” Mirella said.

The clicking followed.

Darius carried the girl under one arm and dragged the travel chest with the other. After twenty paces, a wheel caught between two pipes.

“Leave it,” Lysandra said.

“Everything we own is in this chest.”

“Everything you love is in this tunnel.”

Darius let go.

They ran on without the blankets, the cooking pot, or the spare boots. It is astonishing how little a life weighs once a person must choose what part of it to save.

The trench forked ahead. A blue arrow pointed right. The passage on the left sloped upward toward daylight.

“Right,” Darius said.

They had taken only three steps when the ceiling split.

A Raker dropped through the steam pipes. Darius shoved Lysandra and the girl into the right-hand passage as the creature struck the floor between them and Mirella. Its claws scraped sparks from the stone.

Mirella twisted away with Asher against her chest. She drove her damaged shears toward the Raker's face, but it knocked her arm aside. Darius tried to reach her. A second creature fell behind the first, blocking the fork completely.

“Mirella!” Lysandra cried.

A pipe burst above them with a deafening *BANG*. White steam screamed from the break—*hissssss*—and swallowed the junction.

White steam exploded through the junction. Darius vanished. Mirella heard him shouting, but the words broke apart beneath the scream of metal. She backed into the left passage as the first Raker lunged blindly through the steam.

There was no choice left to make.

She ran uphill with Asher.

Behind her, Darius struck something with the old man's walking stick. A Raker shrieked. Lysandra called Mirella's name once more, farther away now, and then the bent pipe tore free of the ceiling and crashed across the fork.

The two families were separated.

Mirella reached the signal-room door and threw her shoulder against it. The swollen frame refused to move. She struck it again. Asher cried against her neck as claws rang across the fallen pipe below.

On the third blow, the door opened.

The room contained rows of coloured levers, a wall map, and three broad windows overlooking the maintenance yard east of the wall. A small rail engine waited on the outer track with two freight cars and an open passenger wagon. Porters and guards were forcing survivors aboard.

Darius emerged from the right-hand stairwell below. He supported Lysandra with one arm and carried his daughter beneath the other. A Raker came after them.

Conductor Pell stood on the engine step with a coal shovel. He swung it flat against the creature's head—*CLANG!* The Raker reeled, caught the edge of the shovel, and pulled him from the step.

Pell struck the ground hard. Before the creature could fall upon him, Darius drove the iron walking stick through the hollow beneath its jaw. The Raker convulsed. Pell crawled beneath the wagon while two guards finished it with rifle fire.

“Get them aboard!” Darius shouted.

The driver released the brake. Wheels clanked, stopped, then began to turn.

Lysandra reached the passenger wagon. Her daughter had frozen several paces behind her, staring toward the signal-room windows.

For one instant, the girl's eyes met Mirella's across the yard.

Lysandra reached back. “Come on, Sera! We have to go!”

The girl took her mother's hand and climbed aboard.

Darius remained on the rear step. He searched the yard until he saw Mirella at the window.

“The east marker!” he shouted. “We will wait at the marker!”

Mirella could not hear every word through the glass, but she understood. She pressed one hand against the window.

Then the light outside changed.

The ground-level gateway had widened until it filled the road beneath the eastern wall. Rakers spilled from it in twos and threes, racing across the pavement before springing onto roofs, wagons, and fleeing people. One guard fired until his rifle clicked empty, then drew a short blade and placed himself in front of three children hiding beneath a cart. A porter dragged a wounded woman toward the departing engine while another beat at a Raker with a coupling chain. Everywhere Mirella looked, small battles flared and ended.

The greater entity did not join the Rakers' mindless attack.

It walked through the slaughter without hurry. Rakers moved away from its path. Frost spread from every footstep, whitening the ground and climbing anything it touched.

Six soldiers advanced from the gatehouse and fired in pairs. *Crack-crack. Crack-crack.* Their shots split one shoulder plate and tore a strip of darkness from the entity's side. It turned toward them, slower now, and swept one arm through the air.

The temperature fell so quickly that Mirella's breath became a cloud against the window.

Ice sealed the soldiers' rifle actions. One man tore off a glove and tried to clear his weapon. The metal took the skin from his palm. Another soldier fired once more before a spear of frost rose from the pavement and struck him across the chest. The remaining soldiers broke formation.

The entity pursued only far enough to silence them. Frost overtook the fleeing soldiers one after another. When the last rifle stopped, all six lay motionless beneath a whitening crust of ice.

It was not invulnerable. Cracks remained in its armour, and one arm hung lower than before. But there were too few soldiers, their weapons were too scattered, and the Rakers gave them no time to concentrate their fire.

It was still searching.

The pendant against Mirella's skin grew cold enough to burn.

The entity stopped in the centre of the yard.

Its shadowed face turned toward the signal room.

Mirella stepped back from the window.

The little engine was gathering speed. Darius still stood on its rear step, one hand gripping the rail as he watched her. The girl leaned around her mother. Smoke crossed between them.

Mirella lowered her hand.

“They are going,” Asher said.

“Yes.”

“We go too?”

Mirella looked at the black figure crossing the yard. “Not that way.”

The signal room had a second door leading into an old repair shed. She lifted Asher and ran through it.

The shed was long and dim, crowded with broken rail equipment. Chains hung from ceiling beams. Loose wheels stood taller than Asher. At the far end rested a single maintenance carriage, small enough for the narrow service lines beneath the city. Its paint had peeled away, and one side had been opened for repairs.

Mirella carried Asher inside.

The carriage smelled of oil, old wood, and cold iron. Beneath its rear bench she found a maintenance compartment made to hold tools. It was narrow, but deep enough for a child. A slotted metal panel covered the outer side.

She set Asher down and knelt before him.

The red wooden animal remained clenched in his hand.

“Horse,” he said through his tears.

“Yes,” Mirella whispered. “The finest horse in New Babylonia.”

A heavy foot struck the signal-room floor behind them.

*Thud.*

The sound was slow. Measured.

Mirella pulled the pendant from beneath her collar. The metal no longer looked dull. A thin silver light traced the markings across its face, but the metal was rimed with frost. The cold bit into her fingers.

She did not understand why it had awakened. She knew only that the thing in the yard had looked toward them when it did.

Another step sounded.

*Thud.*

This one landed closer, somewhere beyond the signal-room door.

Mirella slipped the cord over Asher's head and tucked the pendant inside his coat. The silver light disappeared against him.

The slow footsteps ceased. In their place came the faint *crisssh* of frost spreading over stone.

Then the entity turned toward the repair shed.

Mirella's face tightened. Whatever she had hoped the pendant might do, it had not hidden them completely.

She knelt beside the open compartment.

“Inside, Asher.”

He looked into the darkness and wrapped both arms around her neck. “Mama too.”

The entity stepped into the repair shed.

*Thud.*

Frost crept beneath the carriage, whitening the rails and crackling across the floor.

Mirella held Asher tightly. She closed her eyes for one precious moment, breathing in the familiar scent of his hair.

“I cannot come with you,” she whispered.

“No.” His fingers tightened in her coat. “Mama comes.”

Mirella pulled back. She placed the wooden horse in one of his hands, then closed his other hand around the pendant beneath his coat.

“I need you to protect these for me.”

Asher shook his head, already crying.

“Listen to me.” Her voice trembled, but she did not allow it to break. “You must hide. You must be silent. No matter what you hear, you stay here.”

“Don't go.”

Mirella kissed his forehead.

“I need you to trust me one more time.”

He stared at her, too young to understand sacrifice but old enough to recognize goodbye.

“I love you,” she said.

She placed him inside before either of them could become brave enough to refuse. Then Mirella closed the panel.

Through its narrow slots, Asher could see the floor of the carriage and a broken strip of the shed beyond. His mother's boots remained in front of him.

The larger entity advanced between the rails.

Each pale foot settled without haste, leaving a print of white frost across the floor. Ice crept over the hanging chains and stiffened the grease along the rails. The temperature fell with every step.

It faced the maintenance carriage.

Mirella picked up a signal flare from the repair shelf. Her hand trembled as she struck it against the iron frame—*scritch, scritch, fwoom*.

Red fire burst into life.

The flare immediately weakened. Its flame shortened, hissing as frost gathered along the metal handle.

Three Rakers crawled into the shed behind the greater creature. They clicked and snapped at one another, eager to rush past it. The creature lifted one long hand. The Rakers went still.

Its attention remained on the carriage.

On Asher.

Mirella stepped between them.

She threw a loose canvas cover over the open side of the carriage. Darkness closed around Asher, but a tear in the cloth left him one crooked view of the shed.

His mother backed away from him.

The entity's head followed her.

Mirella raised the flare. Red light washed over her face, her loosened hair, and the pale scar through her eyebrow. She moved toward the opposite doors, away from the carriage.

The creature took one step after her.

Mirella took another.

The Rakers began to follow, but the greater entity spread its fingers. They crouched where they stood.

It wanted her for itself.

Mirella reached the outer doors. Wind pulled smoke around her and scattered the flare's failing sparks. Beyond her lay the ruined yard, the bodies, and the dark gateway standing beneath the eastern wall.

She looked back.

Asher could not know whether she saw his eye behind the torn canvas. He knew only that she smiled. It was a small smile, the kind she gave when tucking his blanket beneath his chin.

Then she turned away.

Mirella walked into the yard.

The frost-wreathed entity followed.

Asher pressed both hands against the metal panel. The pendant burned with unnatural cold beneath his fingers. Through the tear in the canvas, he watched his mother grow smaller in the red light.

She did not run.

She kept walking, leading the darkness away from him.

“Mama?”
